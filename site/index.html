Wing I — The Written Word
Exhibit I c. 370 BC

Writing

The Panic

“This discovery of yours will create forgetfulness in the learners’ souls, because they will not use their memories.”

— Plato, Phaedrus, recounting a king’s judgment on the invention of writing

The alphabet, Athens feared, was outsourcing the mind. Students would carry knowledge around on scrolls instead of in their heads — the appearance of wisdom without the substance of it.

The Record

Writing did offload memory — and in exchange delivered law, science, history, and every complaint about technology ever preserved. This warning survives for one reason only: somebody wrote it down.

Survived
Exhibit II c. 1454

The Printing Press

The Panic

“The pen is a virgin; the printing press is a whore.”

— Filippo de Strata, Venetian scribe, c. 1474

A century later the Swiss scholar Conrad Gessner was still sounding the alarm, warning that the press had loosed a “confusing and harmful abundance of books” upon the world. Too much information, moving too fast, into the wrong hands. The first content-moderation crisis.

The Record

The abundance never stopped being confusing. Literacy went from privilege to default anyway. The scribes found other work, and several of their complaints were later printed — some sold rather well.

Exhibit III 1797

The Novel

The Panic

“Novel Reading, a Cause of Female Depravity.”

— actual title of an essay in the Monthly Mirror, 1797

Germany called it Lesewut — reading rage. Physicians warned that fiction overheated the imagination, ruined posture, and left readers unfit for real life. Young women were held to be especially at risk from the paperback.

The Record

The condition proved chronic. Book clubs are its support groups. Two centuries on, the novel is prescribed in schools as the wholesome alternative to whatever the newest machine is.

Survived
Wing II — The Machine Age
Exhibit IV 1866

The Railway

The Panic

Victorian physicians warned that travel at unnatural speeds could rattle the spine and derange the nerves. “Railway spine” — described by the surgeon John Eric Erichsen in 1866 — became a genuine medical diagnosis, and the press ran lurid stories of “railway madmen” driven suddenly insane between stations.

The Record

The human frame held together at thirty miles per hour, and at three hundred. The commute, however, remains a documented source of derangement to this day.

Survived
Exhibit V 1858

The Telegraph

The Panic

“Superficial, sudden, unsifted, too fast for the truth.”

— a New York Times editorial’s verdict on telegraphic news, 1858

Why, the editors wondered, would anyone need scraps of news every ten minutes when the mail from Europe arrived, perfectly adequately, every ten days?

The Record

They were describing the telegraph. They accidentally described the next hundred and seventy years. This exhibit is preserved not because the fear was foolish, but because it was merely early.

Survived
Exhibit VI 1876–1900

The Telephone

The Panic

Early users feared shocks along the line, disease traveling through the wires, and — in more than one recorded case — spirits listening in. Some refused to touch the apparatus during thunderstorms. Commentators warned it would end the civilized practice of visiting, and that a person might now be reached, at any hour, by anyone.

The Record

That last fear was entirely correct. Note also that the dread of answering a ringing telephone has outlived every other fear in this case, and is now considered a defining trait of an entire generation.

Survived
Wing III — The Broadcast Century
Exhibit VII 1936

The Radio

The Panic

“[Children] have developed the habit of dividing attention between the humdrum preparation of their school assignments and the compelling excitement of the loudspeaker.”

— The Gramophone, 1936

The wireless set, parents despaired, had made it impossible for a child to simply sit and do one thing.

The Record

This is the oldest complaint about multitasking with media in the museum’s collection, filed roughly seventy years before the smartphone. The children in question recovered fully, grew up, and complained about television.

Survived
Exhibit VIII 1948–1954

Comic Books

The Panic

Psychiatrist Fredric Wertham’s Seduction of the Innocent blamed comics for juvenile delinquency, and the United States Senate convened hearings on the menace of the ten-cent magazine. In several towns, schoolchildren were marched out to feed their collections into public bonfires. The industry, terrified, muzzled itself with the Comics Code.

The Record

No link to delinquency was ever established. The delinquents grew up; some later bought back the comics they had burned, at auction, for astonishing sums. The Code is gone. The Senate has moved on to newer media, several times.

Survived
Exhibit IX 1961–1967

Television

The Panic

“A vast wasteland.”

— Newton Minow, Chairman of the FCC, addressing broadcasters, 1961

Television would rot the mind, corrode the family, and — every parent agreed — sitting too close to the set would ruin your eyes.

The Record

A note of caution for visitors who find this collection too comforting: in 1967, General Electric recalled color sets that were leaking actual X-rays, and the advice to sit well back was, for one model year, medically sound. Occasionally the panic is simply right. The advice outlived the defect by forty years.

Survived
Exhibit X 1979–1985

Dungeons & Dragons

The Panic

A pencil-and-paper game of elves and spreadsheet arithmetic was declared a gateway to devil worship. Advocacy groups formed, 60 Minutes ran a special, and polyhedral dice were confiscated in school districts across America as instruments of the occult.

The Record

Studies found no link to anything except improved vocabulary. The players grew up to run libraries, film franchises, and a substantial share of the technology industry, and remain, on the whole, suspiciously well-organized.

Survived
Exhibit XI 1976 / 1993

Video Games

The Panic

It began in 1976, when an arcade cabinet called Death Race was condemned by the National Safety Council as sick and morbid. By 1993 the United States Senate was holding hearings on Mortal Kombat, and the medium stood accused of manufacturing violence by the quarter.

The Record

The hearings produced a ratings board. The games produced an industry larger than film and music combined. The teenagers produced spreadsheets, mortgages, and children of their own, whose screen time now worries them sick.

Survived
Wing IV — The Connected Age
Exhibit XII 1995

The Internet

The Panic

“The truth is no online database will replace your daily newspaper.”

— Clifford Stoll, “The Internet? Bah!”, Newsweek, 1995

The same year, psychiatrist Ivan Goldberg invented “Internet Addiction Disorder” as a parody of diagnostic manuals. It was promptly taken seriously, which rather proved a point, though nobody agreed on whose.

The Record

The parody diagnosis proved more durable than the daily newspaper. The museum notes, without further comment, that you are reading this exhibit on the fad in question.

Survived
Exhibit XIII 1999

Y2K

The Panic

Two missing digits in old software would, at the stroke of midnight, down the planes, still the power grids, and empty the banks. Families stocked bunkers with generators and canned goods. The world spent hundreds of billions of dollars bracing for its clocks.

The Record

Midnight arrived. The lights stayed on — in no small part because the panic had funded the armies of programmers who fixed the code in time. The most expensive anticlimax in history, and perhaps the only exhibit in this museum that is secretly a success story. The canned goods were quietly eaten over the following decade.

Survived

Exhibit XIV 2007–present

The Smartphone

The Panic

Within a year of its arrival, researchers had coined nomophobia — the fear of being without one’s phone — making this the first exhibit where the technology and the terror of losing it were catalogued simultaneously. “Brain rot” was named Oxford’s Word of the Year in 2024. Parliaments now legislate bedtimes for software.

The Record

Verdict pending. Early findings suggest the phone did not ruin everything; it merely relocated everything, which may or may not amount to the same thing. This exhibit will be updated when its visitors stop looking at it.
